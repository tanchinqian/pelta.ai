Description
Modern enterprises rely heavily on digital infrastructure to support daily business
operations, customer services, and financial transactions. Most global technology
companies operate a complex IT environment consisting of cloud platforms, enterprise
applications, databases, network infrastructure, and financial systems distributed across
multiple regions. Any unexpected system outage, service degradation, or financial
anomaly can directly affect business continuity, customer experience, operational
efficiency, and organisational reputation.
Traditionally, IT support teams respond to incidents only after users report problems or
system monitoring tools trigger alerts. Similarly, financial fraud detection often relies on
predefined business rules that identify suspicious transactions only after they occur.
While these approaches remain important, they are increasingly insufficient because
modern IT environments generate massive volumes of system logs, application metrics,
user activities, and transaction records that are difficult for human operators to analyse in
real time. 
Recent advances in Artificial Intelligence (AI), Machine Learning (ML), and data
analytics provide new opportunities to shift from reactive monitoring to proactive
prediction. Predictive analytics can analyse historical incident records, infrastructure
performance metrics, application logs, and user behaviour to identify patterns that
typically precede system failures. At the same time, anomaly detection algorithms can
continuously monitor financial transactions and operational activities to identify unusual
behaviour that may indicate fraud, security breaches, insider threats, or operational risks
before significant damage occurs.
The challenge is to design an intelligent monitoring and prediction system capable of
integrating operational and financial data from multiple sources, identifying potential
incidents before they escalate, and recommending or automatically executing appropriate
remediation actions.

Key Challenges
1) Integrating Large Volumes of Operational and Financial Data
Enterprise environments generate enormous amounts of data from different sources,
including application logs, server performance metrics, network monitoring systems,
cloud infrastructure, security events, and financial transaction records.nThe challenge is
to design a scalable data pipeline that can collect, clean, integrate, and process structured
and unstructured data while maintaining data quality and supporting near real-time
analysis. Without proper integration, predictive models may miss important relationships
between infrastructure events and financial risks.
2) Accurate Prediction of IT Incidents and Financial Anomalies
Predicting future incidents is significantly more difficult than detecting existing
problems. System failures may be caused by hardware degradation, software defects,
configuration errors, network congestion, or unexpected user behaviour. An effective AI
solution must distinguish genuine risks from normal operational variations while
minimising false positives and false negatives. The predictive models should also provide
explainable results so that IT engineers, financial analysts, and business managers
understand why a particular incident or transaction has been flagged and can confidently
take appropriate action.
3) Automated Response and Decision Support
Detecting incidents early only creates value if organisations can respond quickly and
effectively. However, enterprise environments often involve thousands of interconnected
systems, making manual investigation and remediation slow and resource-intensive. The
challenge is to design an intelligent decision-support system capable of recommending or
automatically executing appropriate remediation actions, such as restarting failed services,
reallocating computing resources, blocking suspicious financial transactions, or escalating
incidents to the appropriate teams. 
Example Use Cases

incident data 
2.Detect anomalous financial transactions and suspicious user behaviour
3.Recommend automated remediation actions
4.Develop a real-time operational risk dashboard

A subscription-based business model allows customers to pay repeatedly for continuous
access to a product or service instead of making a one-time purchase. As more companies
move from selling products once to providing long-term services, customer experience
becomes increasingly important because revenue depends on whether customers continue
using, renewing, or upgrading their subscriptions.
Customer churn represents the rate at which customers stop using a service, and it gives
companies a clear signal to improve customer experience and retention strategies. The
issue with subscription-based services is that customers may cancel or reduce their plans if
they do not see enough value, experience poor service, or feel that the subscription does
not match their actual needs. This creates pressure for companies to understand customer
behaviour more deeply instead of only tracking sales.
Another issue is under-utilized subscriptions, where customers continue paying for a
service but do not use it effectively. This can lead to dissatisfaction because customers
may feel that they are wasting money or not receiving enough benefit from the plan. At
the same time, companies may miss opportunities to recommend better packages, provide
extra support, or upsell relevant services. 
Research on customer churn prediction shows that machine learning can help identify
customers who are most likely to leave before cancellation happens. Smart subscription
and customer experience optimization is a concept meant to use data analytics, AI, and
customer health monitoring to improve retention, engagement, and upselling
opportunities. A smart system can analyze usage patterns, customer activity, support
history, payment behaviour, and feedback to recommend personalized subscription plans,
detect inactive customers, and help businesses take action at the right time.

Key Challenges
1) Data Integration and Customer Behaviour Analysis
Subscription data often comes from many different sources, such as usage records,
payment history, service requests, customer feedback, and account activity. These data
points may be stored in different systems and may not follow the same format. This
presents the challenge of designing a system that can collect, clean, and connect customer
information into one complete profile so that businesses can understand customer
behaviour accurately.
2) Accurate Churn Prediction and Customer Health Scoring
A major challenge is identifying which customers are likely to cancel, downgrade, or stop
engaging with the service. Churn is not always obvious because a customer may still be
subscribed but may already be dissatisfied or inactive. The system must analyze warning
signs such as reduced usage, repeated complaints, late payments, low engagement, or
unused features. This requires predictive models that are accurate, explainable, and useful
for customer service, sales, and management teams.
3) Personalization and Scalable Customer Experience
Different customers have different needs depending on their usage level, budget, business
goals, and service expectations. A smart subscription system must be able to recommend
suitable plans, support actions, or upselling opportunities without making the process
too complicated. The challenge is to balance automation with human decision-making so
that the company can deliver a more personalized experience while still managing many
customers efficiently.
Example Use Cases
1.Predict customer churn based on usage patterns
2.Recommend personalized subscription plans
3.Identify inactive or under-utilized subscriptions
4.Build a dashboard to monitor customer health and engagement

Description
As organisations embed generative AI and machine learning tools into everyday workflows,
the pace of adoption is beginning to outrun the policies meant to control it. Employees
now reach for AI assistants for coding, drafting, analysis, and customer communication
long before IT, legal, and compliance teams have agreed on what is safe to share with these
tools. This creates a governance gap: AI is generating real productivity gains, but often
without the oversight, transparency, or accountability that would normally accompany a
new enterprise system.
The scale of this gap is significant. Survey data collected in early 2026 found that nearly half
of employees use AI tools their employer has not approved, with a majority of free-tool
users doing so without any enterprise-grade data protection. This so-called “shadow AI”
problem is not confined to junior staff either; a large share of C-suite executives say they
tolerate unapproved AI use because the productivity gains are too valuable to slow down.
The financial exposure is measurable too: breaches involving AI-related tools now carry a
substantial cost premium over conventional data breaches, driven largely by slower
detection and limited forensic visibility into what data entered a given AI tool.
The challenge is to design a system or framework that ensures AI governance, including
data protection, transparency, and ethical use. In practice, this means knowing which AI
tools are in use, who is allowed to use them, what data they can see, and how their outputs
get checked. Frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework organise
responsible AI practice around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage, and
are increasingly used as the practical backbone underneath emerging regulation such as the
EU AI Act. Access to a given AI model is also not guaranteed to stay the same. In June
2026, the U.S. government briefly ordered Anthropic to suspend access to two of its
newest models, Claude Fable 5 and Claude Mythos 5, for every foreign national
worldwide, before lifting the restriction weeks later. The trigger was a cybersecurity
concern: Amazon researchers reported a way to jailbreak Fable 5's safeguards, prompting
the model to identify software vulnerabilities and, in one case, produce exploit code.

Key Challenges
1) Ensuring Responsible AI Usage, and Employee Compliance
Employees adopt new AI tools far faster than governance processes can be written for them,
and outright bans tend to push usage further out of sight rather than eliminating it. A
workable system needs to make the approved path the easiest path: a new AI tool needs to
be approvable in days, not months, while still capturing what data it will touch and who is
at risk. The primary difficulty here is organisational and behavioural as much as technical,
the system must change how thousands of employees across departments behave, not just
process a data feed. This is a matter of building willing compliance, not just introducing
regulations.
2) Data Privacy and Security
AI tools make it possible to move data outside an organisation’s control with a single click,
because prompts, documents, and outputs can be pasted into a chat window in seconds. A
simple solution that comes to mind is that the framework must classify data sensitivity,
restrict which categories of information may be shared with which class of AI tool, and
maintain a record of AI usage that can satisfy both internal audit at the company and
external government regulators. This differs from a conventional cybersecurity monitoring
problem in that the “attack surface” is often an authorised employee using an unauthorised
tool, rather than an external adversary. 
3) Transparency and Ethical Boundaries in AI Use
Being transparent and ethical means more than avoiding bias or technical mistakes, it's
about whether someone affected by an AI-assisted decision can find out AI was involved,
and get a plain explanation for the outcome as well as a redressal pathway. Where an AI tool
helps decide something that affects a person, that person should be able to learn that AI
played a part and ask for the decision to be looked at again. This also means setting clear
limits on what AI should never be used for, even where it's technically possible such as
covertly monitoring employees or profiling people without their knowledge.
Example Use Cases
1.Build framework to monitor AI model risks (Bias and Data Leakage)
2.Implement approval workflow for AI tool usage
3.Develop dashboard to track AI usage across departments
4.Suggest policies or automated checks for sensitive data
